Soldering tools, solder and safety for beginners

You don't need a lab. A temperature-controlled iron, the right solder and a few hand tools will take you a long way, and a handful of habits keeps your lungs, eyes and fingers out of trouble.

The starter kit

What's worth having from day one, and why:

  • A temperature-controlled soldering iron, with a stand. The one tool not to skimp on.
  • A chisel tip about 2 to 3 mm wide; a smaller one can come later.
  • Solder: 0.8 mm wire with a flux core (more below).
  • A tip cleaner: brass wool in a holder, or a damp sponge.
  • Flush cutters to trim leads close to the joint.
  • Small pliers for bending leads and holding hot things.
  • Wire strippers that handle thin wire (22 to 30 AWG).
  • Desoldering braid and a solder sucker, for taking solder off again.
  • Something to hold the board: "helping hands", a PCB holder or a small vise.
  • A multimeter with a continuity beeper, for checking your work.
  • Safety glasses, and a fan or fume extractor to move smoke away from your face.
  • Isopropyl alcohol and an old toothbrush for cleaning flux off.

Nice to have: a flux pen, tweezers, a magnifier or loupe, a heat-resistant silicone mat and some heat-shrink tubing.

Choosing an iron and tip

The feature that matters is temperature control: you set a temperature and the iron holds it, putting heat back as fast as each joint soaks it up. Cheap fixed-power irons that plug straight into the wall run either too cool to flow the solder or hot enough to cook it, and they cause a lot of "soldering is hard" frustration. A bench station and a modern pen-style iron powered over USB-C are both good choices.

Power helps the iron recover between joints. Adafruit suggests at least 25 W for building kits, and most decent temperature-controlled irons have plenty more than that.

For the tip, pick a chisel (screwdriver) shape about 2 to 3 mm across. Its flat face touches the pad and the lead at the same time and passes heat quickly. Fine needle points look precise, but they carry little heat, which makes through-hole work slower and harder.

Choosing solder

Electronics solder is a thin wire with flux in its core. Never use plumbing solder or acid flux on electronics: the acid corrodes it.

Leaded solder is the friendliest to learn with, and fine for hobby use if you wash your hands afterwards (see safety). Lead-free is the better choice if you'd rather avoid lead altogether, if children share the space, or if you're repairing modern equipment, which is built lead-free. It just takes a hotter iron and a little more practice.

Diameter: 0.8 mm is the all-round size for through-hole work; 0.5 mm suits small joints. Thicker solder floods small joints before you can stop it.

What temperature?

Start around 330 °C for leaded solder and 360 °C for lead-free, then adjust. The right temperature is the lowest one that makes a joint flow in two or three seconds. Big joints, thick wires and wide copper soak up heat and need more; small pads need less.

  • 217–220 °CSAC305 lead-free solder melts
  • 320–350 °CThe iron, for leaded solder
  • 350–380 °CThe iron, for lead-free solder
  • ~350 °C, 3 sA typical datasheet limit for an iron on a part's lead (this one from an LED)
  • 400 °C +Too hot for everyday through-hole work: flux burns, tips wear out, pads lift

Hotter isn't faster. Too much heat burns the flux off before it can work, blackens the tip and lifts pads off the board. If you find yourself turning the iron up and up, check that the tip is clean and tinned, and that it touches both the pad and the lead.

Flux, the secret ingredient

Hot metal oxidizes almost instantly, and solder won't stick to oxide. Flux cleans the surfaces as they heat up and lets the molten solder wet them: spread out and bond, instead of balling up. The core of your solder carries enough for ordinary joints, but it burns away within seconds, which is why you feed solder into the joint instead of carrying a blob over on the tip.

Extra flux, from a pen or a syringe, is a big help for reworking old joints, clearing bridges and desoldering. Fluxes come in three families:

  • Rosin (from pine resin): the classic. Clean its sticky brown residue off with isopropyl alcohol and a brush.
  • No-clean: leaves little residue, which can stay on, though many people clean it off anyway.
  • Water-soluble: strong, and its residue is corrosive, so it must be washed off thoroughly. Best avoided on hobby boards.

Looking after the tip

A clean, shiny, tinned tip passes heat into a joint. A black, dry one doesn't, and no temperature setting will make up for it. Keep it in shape:

  • Tin it as soon as it's hot: melt on a little solder so the whole working face is silver.
  • Wipe it on brass wool every joint or two. A sponge works too if it's damp, not soaking, but brass wool doesn't chill the tip.
  • Re-tin it before putting it down, and leave a blob on when you switch off. The solder keeps air off the tip.
  • Turn it down or off when it's idle. Tips oxidize fastest when they sit hot and dry.
  • Never file or sand a plated tip. If it's black and won't take solder, scrub it on brass wool and re-tin it, or use tip tinner (a little tin of tinning paste).

Staying safe

Soldering is a safe hobby with a few real hazards. These habits cover them.

Fumes: it's the flux

The wisp of smoke from a joint is flux, not metal. Rosin flux fume irritates the airways, and the UK's Health and Safety Executive names it a common cause of occupational asthma in people who solder for a living. You'll solder far less, but there's no reason to breathe it.

  • Keep your face out of the plume rising from the joint.
  • Use a fan to draw the smoke away from you, toward an open window if you can.
  • A small bench-top extractor with a carbon filter is mostly a fan: it moves the plume away from your face, but cleans little of it, so a window still helps.
  • Work in a ventilated room, and take breaks.

Lead: it's your hands

Lead doesn't evaporate at soldering temperatures (it boils at over 1,700 °C), so the risk from leaded solder isn't in the air. It's lead on your fingers finding its way into your mouth.

  • Wash your hands after soldering, and before you eat, drink or smoke.
  • Keep food and drink off the bench.
  • Keep solder and offcuts away from children and pets, and wipe the bench down.

Burns: respect the iron

  • The tip is around 350 °C. Always put the iron back in its stand, never down on the bench.
  • If you drop it, let it fall. Never try to catch an iron.
  • Route the cable where you can't snag it, and switch off when you leave (many irons sleep by themselves).
  • For a burn, cool it under cool running water for 20 minutes, and get medical help for anything bigger than a small spot.

Eyes: leads fly

Snipped leads shoot off at a surprising speed, and solder can spit when it hits a wet sponge or a bubble of flux. Wear safety glasses, and when you trim a lead, hold its end or point it down at the bench.

Dust and mains

  • Cutting or sanding glass-fiber board (FR-4) makes fine glass dust. Score and snap it instead, and wear a mask if you must sand.
  • Keep early projects low voltage: batteries, USB and plug-in adapters. Circuits that connect to mains electricity don't belong on perfboard until you really know what you're doing.

Setting up your bench

  • Bright light from more than one direction, so shadows don't hide the joints.
  • A heat-resistant surface: a silicone mat, or a board you don't mind marking.
  • The iron on the side of your writing hand, its stand where you can reach it without looking.
  • A fan or extractor pulling the smoke away from your face.
  • The board held firmly at a comfortable angle, leaving both hands free: one for the iron, one for the solder.
  • A small dish for offcuts, so they end up there and not inside your next circuit.

Frequently asked questions

Should a beginner use leaded or lead-free solder?

Leaded solder (63/37 or 60/40) is easier to learn with: it melts at a lower temperature and flows more readily. Lead-free solder such as SAC305 works fine with a temperature-controlled iron set a little hotter. If you use leaded solder, wash your hands afterwards and keep food off the bench.

What temperature should my soldering iron be?

Start around 330 °C for leaded solder and 360 °C for lead-free, then use the lowest setting that makes a joint flow within two or three seconds. Bigger joints need more heat, small ones less.

What wattage soldering iron do I need?

Temperature control matters more than watts. Any decent temperature-controlled iron of 25 W or more handles through-hole work; more power just recovers heat faster on big joints.

Are solder fumes dangerous?

The smoke is mostly burnt flux, which irritates the airways and, with long-term heavy exposure, can cause asthma. Keep your face out of the plume and use a fan or extractor to move it away. Lead doesn't vaporize at soldering temperatures; wash your hands so you don't swallow any.
